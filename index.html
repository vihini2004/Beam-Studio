    1. Supports and reactions (slides 14–18)

    A support stops some movements of the beam. For every movement it stops, it supplies one reaction: a force if it stops a translation, a moment if it stops a rotation. The same list tells you the boundary conditions you will need later for deflection.

    A free end has no support: there V = 0 and M = 0.

    2. Static determinacy and stability (slides 12–13)

    A beam in a plane gives three equilibrium equations: ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0. Each internal hinge gives one more, because the moment there must be zero. Let r be the number of reaction components and c the number of hinges:

    • r < 3 + c: unstable. There are too few reactions and the beam moves as a mechanism.
    • r = 3 + c: statically determinate, provided the supports are sensibly arranged. Statics alone gives every reaction.
    • r > 3 + c: statically indeterminate to degree r − (3 + c). The extra equations come from deformation (compatibility), which is why these beams need EI.

    Counting is not enough on its own. If every reaction is parallel (rollers only) the beam cannot resist a sideways push, even when r = 3. This is an improper constraint. Under purely vertical loads ΣFx = 0 holds on its own, so beams resting on rollers or knife edges can still be analysed for vertical loads. The studio says so when it happens. A hinge that leaves one piece with only one support is also a mechanism.

    Try it: load Continuous. Then change the pinned support to a roller, add a hinge, or delete a support, and watch the badge above the diagrams.

    3. Equivalent loads (slides 21–22)

    For reactions, a distributed load can be replaced by a single force equal to its area, acting at its centroid.

    • Uniform load (UDL) w over length a: W = wa, acting at the middle.
    • Triangular load (UVL) from 0 to w: W = ½wa, acting at a/3 from the heavy end.
    • Trapezoidal load: split it into a rectangle and a triangle and add the two.

    Do not use the resultant when you work out V and M at a section. There, only the part of the load to the left of the cut counts. For a UDL, w(x − a) acts at (x − a)/2 from the cut.

    4. Shear force and bending moment: sign convention

    Cut the beam at a distance x from the left end. The two pieces act on each other with an internal shear force V and bending moment M, equal and opposite on the two faces.

    • V is positive when the resultant of the forces on the left part acts upward. The left part tends to slide up past the right part.
    • M is positive (sagging) when the moments of the forces on the left part about the cut are clockwise. The beam bends into a smile: the top is squeezed (compression) and the bottom is stretched (tension). Negative M is hogging, a frown.

    So for any section: V = Σ(upward forces on the left) and M = Σ(clockwise moments of the left forces about the cut). Cut a section in the studio by clicking a diagram. It draws that free-body diagram with the numbers filled in.

    5. How load, shear and moment are related

    Look at a short piece of beam of length dx carrying a downward load w per metre:

    Vertical equilibrium:  V − w dx − (V + dV) = 0  ⇒  dVdx = −w
    Moments about the right face:  M + V dx − w dx²/2 − (M + dM) = 0  ⇒  dMdx = V

    These two lines explain every shape you will see:

    • The slope of the SFD is −w. With no load the SFD is flat; under a UDL it slopes down steadily.
    • The slope of the BMD is V. Where V > 0 the BMD rises; where V < 0 it falls.
    • So M is a maximum or minimum where V = 0, or where V jumps through zero under a point load.
    • Integrating: MB − MA = (area of the SFD between A and B). This is the area method.
    • A point load P makes V jump by P and puts a corner in the BMD. A couple C makes M jump by C and leaves V unchanged.

    6. Drawing SFD and BMD by hand

    1. Find all the reactions (ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0, plus compatibility if the beam is indeterminate).
    2. Mark every key point: supports, point loads, couples, and the ends of distributed loads.
    3. Start at the left end with V = 0 and M = 0 and move right. At each key point, apply the jumps. Between key points, use the shapes in the table.
    4. Get M at each key point from the area of the SFD. Find the peak moment where V = 0.
    5. Check: both diagrams must return to zero at the right end. If they do not, a reaction is wrong.

    A point of contraflexure is where M changes sign. There the beam changes from sagging to hogging, and the deflected shape has an inflection point.

    7. Deflection: the elastic curve

    A bending moment curves the beam. For a linearly elastic beam (Hooke's law, σ = Eε), the curvature is proportional to the moment: 1/ρ = M/EI. For small slopes the curvature is d²y/dx², which gives the equation of the elastic curve:

    EI d²ydx² = M(x)
    • E is Young's modulus of the material (steel ≈ 200 GPa, concrete ≈ 30 GPa, timber ≈ 10 GPa).
    • I is the second moment of area of the cross-section about its neutral axis. For a rectangle b × h, I = bh³/12.
    • The product EI is the flexural rigidity, the beam's resistance to bending. Double EI and every deflection halves.
    • Here y is measured upward, so sagging (M > 0) makes the curve concave up and a loaded beam's y is negative. Books that measure y downward write EI y″ = −M. The physics is the same.

    Integrating once gives EI·(slope); integrating again gives EI·y. Each integration adds a constant, and the supports fix them: y = 0 at every support, and slope = 0 at a fixed support. For an indeterminate beam, the same conditions also give the extra reactions.

    Units check: EI in kN·m² = E in GPa × I in 10⁶ mm⁴. For example, 200 × 100 = 20 000 kN·m².

    8. Macaulay's method

    Writing a separate M(x) for every segment gives two constants per segment. Macaulay's method writes one expression for the whole beam, so there are only two constants, C1 and C2.

    1. Put the origin at the left end. Write M(x) for a section in the last segment, including every force to its left, as a Macaulay bracket ⟨x − a⟩.
    2. A bracket is zero whenever x < a. That switches each term on only after its force has been passed.
    3. Integrate brackets as a whole, never expanding them: ∫⟨x − a⟩n dx = ⟨x − a⟩n+1/(n + 1).
    4. A point load P at a gives −P⟨x − a⟩. A UDL starting at a gives −w⟨x − a⟩²/2. A clockwise couple C gives +C⟨x − a⟩⁰.
    5. A UDL that stops at b: let it run to the end, then add an equal upward UDL from b, giving +w⟨x − b⟩²/2.
    6. Find C1 and C2 from the boundary conditions. The maximum deflection is where dy/dx = 0, or at a free end.

    The Worked solution tab applies exactly these steps to your beam, showing every term and constant.
